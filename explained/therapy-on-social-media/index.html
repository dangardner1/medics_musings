Explained · the real medicine behind the jokes

Is TikTok therapy real therapy?

Unpack your childhood in 30 seconds, set to a trending sound, then swipe up for a discount on a journal. It's the fastest therapy ever invented, and it has the outcomes to match.

The short answer

No. Short mental health videos can reduce stigma, help people name what they're feeling, and nudge them toward real care, and those are genuinely good things. But a video isn't therapy: it doesn't know you, can't assess you, and is optimized for watch time, not for getting you better. The algorithm is a great listener, in the sense that it never stops listening.

The misinformation problem

Reviews of popular mental health videos have repeatedly found that a large share contain misleading or oversimplified claims, and many creators have no clinical training. Normal experiences get packaged as symptoms (“if you like your coffee a certain way, you might have trauma”), and a checklist of relatable feelings gets passed off as a diagnosis. Self-diagnosing from a 30-second clip is like doing your own surgery because you watched the play-by-play.

Therapy words, overworked

Clinical terms have gone viral and lost their meaning along the way: trauma, narcissist, boundaries, triggered and gaslighting, which Merriam-Webster named its word of the year in 2022. The term comes from Patrick Hamilton's 1938 play Gas Light, about a husband who manipulates his wife into doubting her own perceptions. It describes a specific pattern of manipulation, not a partner who forgot to take out the trash.

How to use it without getting used

Treat mental health content as a starting point, not a diagnosis. Check whether the creator is a licensed professional, be wary of anyone selling a cure in the comments, and bring what resonates to an actual clinician. If you're in crisis in the U.S., call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. It is, notably, free, and there's no subscription.

Frequently asked questions

Is mental health advice on TikTok reliable?

Some is helpful, but reviews have found a large share of popular mental health videos contain misleading or oversimplified claims, and many creators aren't clinically trained. Treat it as a starting point, not a diagnosis.

Can you diagnose yourself from social media?

No. Relatable symptom lists can't replace an assessment by a licensed clinician who knows your history.

Where does the word gaslighting come from?

From Patrick Hamilton's 1938 play Gas Light, in which a husband manipulates his wife into doubting her own perceptions. Merriam-Webster named gaslighting its 2022 word of the year.
