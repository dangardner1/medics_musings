What are defense mechanisms?

The mind's security guards, working unpaid overtime to keep uncomfortable feelings out of the building. Some are sophisticated. Denial is not, but it's convinced it is.

The short answer

Defense mechanisms are mostly unconscious strategies the mind uses to protect itself from anxiety, painful feelings or unacceptable thoughts. Everyone uses them, every day. The idea began with Sigmund Freud and was organized most famously by his daughter Anna Freud in her 1936 book The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence, proving that defense mechanisms, like dental records, run in families.

The usual suspects

Denial: refusing to accept something is happening (“it's just a little chest pressure”). Projection: attributing your own unwanted feelings to someone else (“you're the one who's angry”). Displacement: taking feelings out on a safer target, like yelling at the printer instead of the department chair. Rationalization: inventing a sensible reason after the fact (“I skipped the gym to rest my joints”). Reaction formation: acting the opposite of what you feel, such as being conspicuously sweet to a colleague you can't stand.

The grown-up ones

The psychiatrist George Vaillant, who studied how people adapted over decades of adult life, ranked defenses from immature to mature. Among the most mature: sublimation (channeling impulses into something useful, like surgery or competitive pickleball), altruism, suppression (consciously deciding to deal with it later), anticipation and humor. Which means the whole premise of our Freud episode, turning burnout into stand-up, is technically a promotion up the defense hierarchy.

Why therapists care

Defenses aren't bad; they get us through the day. They become a problem when they're rigid or cost more than they save, like denial that delays needed care, or projection that wrecks relationships. Therapy aims to notice them, not confiscate them. You get to keep your sense of humor.

Frequently asked questions

What are examples of defense mechanisms?

Common ones include denial, projection, displacement, rationalization, reaction formation, intellectualization, sublimation and humor.

Who came up with defense mechanisms?

Sigmund Freud introduced the idea, and Anna Freud described them systematically in her 1936 book The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence.

Is humor a defense mechanism?

Yes, and a healthy one. Psychiatrist George Vaillant classified humor, along with sublimation and altruism, as a mature defense.
