How does robotic surgery work?

The robot doesn't operate on you. A surgeon does, from across the room, in what Dr. Gordon calls a high-tech romance gone digital. The robot just gets the credit on the billboard.

The short answer

In robotic-assisted surgery, the surgeon sits at a console, often a few feet from you, and controls robotic arms that hold a camera and small instruments inserted through keyhole incisions. The robot makes no decisions and moves nothing on its own; every motion is the surgeon's. It is, essentially, a very expensive set of extremely steady hands.

What the robot brings to the party

A magnified, three-dimensional view; instruments with “wrists” that bend more than a human hand could at the end of a long stick; motion scaling; and tremor filtering, so the surgeon's morning coffee stays out of the operation. That makes some tight, delicate work, like operating deep in the pelvis, easier than with standard laparoscopic tools.

What it takes away

Distance, and for most of its history, touch. Early systems gave surgeons little or no force feedback, so they learned to judge tissue by sight alone. Newer systems have started adding force feedback, but the surgeon's hands are still no longer in the patient, which is the heartbreak at the center of Out of Touch.

Is it actually better?

For some operations it offers real advantages; for others, large studies show outcomes similar to standard laparoscopy at higher cost and sometimes longer operating time. The surgeon's experience with the specific operation matters more than the machine. Hospitals may advertise the robot; the surgeon is the one you're really hiring.

Frequently asked questions

Does a robot perform robotic surgery?

No. A surgeon controls every movement from a console; the robot holds and moves the instruments. Nobody is getting operated on by a toaster.

Can surgeons feel tissue during robotic surgery?

Traditionally very little. Most systems rely on visual cues; some newer instruments add force feedback.

What should I ask my surgeon about robotic surgery?

How many of this specific operation they've done robotically, why they recommend it over laparoscopic or open surgery for you, and what their complication and conversion rates are.
