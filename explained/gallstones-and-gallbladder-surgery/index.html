Gallstones and gallbladder surgery, explained

The official sport of Fourth and Gall, and the entire livelihood of the (fictional, hard-working) International Brotherhood of Biliary Tract Workers.

The short answer

Gallstones are hardened deposits, usually cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder, a small pouch under the liver that stores bile. Plenty of people have gallstones and never find out; the stones just sit there like a rock collection nobody asked for. Trouble starts when one blocks the flow of bile.

How they announce themselves

Biliary colic is a steady, often severe pain in the upper right or middle of the abdomen, sometimes spreading to the right shoulder or back, frequently after a fatty meal (the gallbladder has opinions about cheeseburgers) and lasting from about 30 minutes to several hours. Fever, pain that won't quit, yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), dark urine or pale stools suggest a complication and need prompt medical attention.

When stones start causing real trouble

Acute cholecystitis is inflammation of the gallbladder from a stuck stone. A stone that escapes into the common bile duct (choledocholithiasis) can cause jaundice, pancreatitis, or cholangitis, an infection of the bile ducts classically signaled by fever, jaundice and right upper abdominal pain (Charcot's triad). Mirizzi syndrome, a stone in the gallbladder neck pressing on the main bile duct, is rarer. In the show's telling, the Brotherhood named a home for the laparoscopically insane after it; in real life it's just a very inconvenient syndrome.

The eviction process

Gallstones that cause symptoms are usually treated by removing the gallbladder, most often laparoscopically (laparoscopic cholecystectomy). You can live perfectly well without it; it's more of a storage closet than a load-bearing wall. Stones in the bile duct may be removed endoscopically before or after surgery (ERCP) or during the operation by exploring the duct, which is the play-by-play action in Fourth and Gall. Surgeons may take an X-ray of the ducts during the operation (an intraoperative cholangiogram) to check for stragglers.

Frequently asked questions

Do gallstones always need surgery?

No. Gallstones that cause no symptoms usually don't need treatment. Surgery is generally recommended once they start causing pain or complications.

Can you live without a gallbladder?

Yes. Bile flows straight from the liver into the intestine. Some people notice looser stools after fatty meals, which usually improves.

What is ERCP?

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, which is why everyone just says ERCP. A scope passed through the mouth into the small intestine lets a specialist reach the bile duct opening and remove stones without an incision.
