What is prior authorization?

The paperwork your insurer wants filed before it pays for the care your doctor already said you need. It's the reason this show's tagline is “no prior authorization required.”

The short answer

Prior authorization (also called pre-authorization, pre-certification or just “prior auth”) is a rule in many health plans: before the plan will pay for certain tests, drugs, procedures or equipment, your clinician has to ask the plan for approval and show that the care is medically necessary.

If the plan says yes, the care is covered under your benefits. If it says no, you can still get the care, but you may be paying for it, and your clinician can appeal.

What usually needs it

It varies by plan, but the usual suspects are advanced imaging (MRI, CT, PET), specialty and brand-name drugs, planned surgeries and hospital stays, durable medical equipment, home health, and some physical therapy. Emergency care should not require prior authorization.

How it works (in theory)

Your clinician's office submits a request with your diagnosis, the planned care and supporting notes. The plan approves it, denies it, or asks for more information. If it's denied, the clinician can often request a “peer-to-peer” call with a doctor who works for the plan, then file a formal appeal.

In practice this involves fax machines more often than anyone would like to admit, and hold music long enough to qualify as a residency.

Why doctors complain about it

Insurers say prior authorization curbs unnecessary and unsafe care. Physicians say it delays treatment and eats hours of staff time every week; American Medical Association surveys consistently find that most physicians believe it sometimes leads patients to abandon treatment. It is one of the most-cited drivers of administrative burden and burnout.

What changed in 2026

A federal rule from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS-0057-F) requires Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP and federal Marketplace plans to decide prior authorization requests within 72 hours for urgent requests and 7 calendar days for standard ones, starting in 2026, and to give a specific reason when they deny. Employer plans aren't covered by that rule, and many states have their own “gold card” and timeline laws.

What you can do as a patient

Ask your clinician's office whether the test or drug needs prior authorization and whether it's been submitted. Get the reference number. Call your plan to check the status. If it's denied, ask for the denial in writing and the reason, ask your clinician about a peer-to-peer review, and file an appeal: many denials are overturned on appeal.

Frequently asked questions

How long does prior authorization take?

Anywhere from minutes (electronic approvals) to several weeks. For Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP and federal Marketplace plans, federal rules from 2026 require a decision within 72 hours for urgent requests and 7 calendar days for standard ones.

Who is responsible for getting prior authorization?

Usually the ordering clinician's office submits it, but it's your coverage on the line, so it's worth confirming it was requested and approved before a planned test or procedure.

What should I do if prior authorization is denied?

Ask for the denial and reason in writing, ask your clinician to request a peer-to-peer review, and file an appeal with your plan. If the plan upholds the denial, you can usually request an external review by an independent reviewer.

Does emergency care need prior authorization?

No. Emergency care should not require prior authorization. In an emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.
