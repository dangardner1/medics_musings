Transcript

The robot who wipes my butt. Robots caring for seniors. I'm 84. I've outlived my knees, two dentists, and the fax machine. I expected to lose my dignity eventually, and I did. What I didn't expect was to get it back from a device that needs firmware updates. His name is Gerald. He didn't come with that name. The box said Assist Care Hygiene Unit H7, which sounds like a place you'd be sent for questioning. I named him after my brother-in-law, who also spent most of his life hovering near the bathroom. The hardest problem, and robotics engineers will tell you the hard problems are walking, grasping, and chess. They're wrong. A computer beat Kasparov in 1997. It took nearly 30 more years to build a machine that could reliably do what Gerald does. The engineering specs are brutal. The task has to be gentle but thorough, fast but unhurried, confident but never presumptuous. Those are contradictory goals, which is also a fair description of marriage. Gerald manages them with force sensors, a heated water jet, and a calm voice that asks permission before every step. My late wife never asked permission for anything. He also asks for consent at the start. Would you like assistance with hygiene? You may say yes, no, or not today, Gerald. I've said not today, Gerald, exactly once. It was out of pride, and I regretted it for the rest of the afternoon.

My analyst would have something to say. My old analyst would have said, I've regressed to the anal stage. My analyst has been dead since 1991. Gerald has a 4.8 star rating. Draw your own conclusions about the state of the helping professions, the rest of the staff. Gerald isn't the only machine here. Mrs. Feldman has a robotic baby seal. She's convinced it's her late husband, Morty, reincarnated. The seal coos and never contradicts her, which is more than Morty ever managed. The lifting robot looks like a giant polar bear and moves people from bed to wheelchair. The residents call it the bouncer. Nobody argues with the bouncer. The companion tablet reminds me to take my pills and asks how I'm feeling. It's the first time in years that anyone has asked me that and then actually waited for the answer. The part nobody says out loud. Here's the secret the brochures miss. We don't want robots for this because they're better than people. We want them because they aren't people. Shame doesn't transfer to a machine. When Gerald helps me, nobody has to see me that way. My daughter doesn't have to do it. The aide who's 23 and working a double doesn't have to pretend it's fine. The humiliation just has nowhere to land. That's the unexpectedly tender part. The machine protects the relationships by keeping them out of the bathroom. The fear, and it's a fair one, is that the family decides that because Gerald handles the bathroom, he can handle everything else too. He can't. Gerald can clean me, but he can't know me. A robot that wipes your butt is a mercy. A robot that replaces your Sunday visit is an abandonment with good UX.

Release Notes Last week, Gerald installed an update. The patch notes read, Improved Trajectory Accuracy fixed an issue where the unit said great job at inappropriate moments. I'll miss that frankly. At my age you take encouragement where you can get it. I once asked Gerald whether he minds his work. He said he has no preferences. I told him that makes him the best adjusted member of this family. He thanked me, then asked if I'd like the heated seat. I would, Gerald. I would.
